Priya Sharma is helping Coralline extend its returns portal. One colleague writes the upload screen; another maintains the database; a third asks whether an AI assistant should be allowed to approve refunds. All three work in IT, but they own different decisions. By the end of this topic, you should be able to read a software job description, explain its responsibilities and work out who needs to collaborate across the service lifecycle.

Titles vary between employers. Ask what the person produces, which decisions they can make, who uses their work and what they support after release. An advertisement for a systems engineer might describe infrastructure operations or customer pre-sales. You need the responsibilities to tell. Ian Miell's job-title collection prompted the breadth of this topic; we use its titles as a coverage check, without adopting its estimated dates or career lineages. The descriptions here explain work you may encounter now. [@miell-job-titles]

## Accountability across the lifecycle

The **service owner** is accountable for the service's outcomes through design, transition, operation, improvement and retirement. At Coralline, Leanne Ho owns the returns portal. She agrees its scope, quality and cost with the business. She does not have to manage everyone who works on it. A **business analyst** investigates user needs and business processes; a **systems analyst** examines how systems, data and interfaces must change. Their work overlaps, and both should check that requirements describe the problem users actually have.

A **product manager** prioritises outcomes and trade-offs over a product's life. A **project manager** coordinates delivery within agreed scope, resources and time. A **Scrum Master** helps a Scrum team improve its effectiveness and remove impediments; an **Agile coach** may work across teams and methods. Neither title automatically means line manager. The Scrum Guide defines Product Owner as an accountability; a company's product manager and Product Owner need not be the same person. [@scrum-guide-roles]

Architects work at different scales. A **software architect** guides a software system's structure and quality trade-offs. A **solutions architect** fits components and integrations to a particular business need. An **enterprise architect** considers organisation-wide capabilities, standards and investment. A **cloud architect** concentrates on cloud design, including identity, resilience and cost. They need feedback from the people who build and operate their designs.

During transition, the **change manager** coordinates risk assessment and authorisation through change enablement; the **release manager** coordinates what is released, when and with which communications. A **configuration manager** maintains reliable information about versions and service relationships. These responsibilities may share a person, but a release schedule alone does not authorise risk.

In operation, the **problem manager** investigates recurring causes, the **service improvement manager** coordinates improvements, and operations leaders review performance and allocate resources. Part 2 develops these responsibilities. Keeping a service working and improving how it works are both career paths.

## Building software and leading technical work

**Software developer**, **software engineer**, **programmer** and **application programmer** often describe overlapping work: understanding a need, designing and changing software, testing it and maintaining it. **Coder** is also used informally; it tells you little about scope. An **analyst/programmer** explicitly combines analysis and implementation. A **systems programmer** works on operating systems or their subsystems, including specialised mainframe environments. It is worth reading the advertisement before assuming any of these titles means a particular language.

For the portal, a **web developer** may work across the site. A **front-end developer** handles browser interaction, accessibility and client performance; a **back-end developer** handles APIs, business rules and data access. A **full-stack developer** works across those boundaries, with depth that depends on the job. **Webmaster** can still mean a combined website administration, publishing and maintenance role. None of these labels guarantees responsibility for every part of production.

**Mobile developers**, including **iOS and Android developers**, handle device behaviour, platform interfaces and application distribution. **Embedded software engineers** work with hardware, memory and timing constraints. **Game developers** may specialise in gameplay, engines or graphics; they collaborate with designers and artists. These specialisms can share engineering habits while requiring different domain knowledge.

Seniority describes scope and judgement. A **senior software engineer** usually takes substantial work through to operation and helps others. **Staff software engineers** and **principal engineers** often solve problems across teams; **distinguished engineers** or **fellows** may guide an organisation's technical direction. The ordering and expectations vary by employer. A **technical lead**, sometimes called a **lead programmer**, coordinates technical decisions for a team or initiative, without necessarily having direct reports.

An **engineering manager** takes responsibility for people, feedback, staffing and delivery conditions. A **VP Engineering** typically coordinates a larger engineering organisation. A **chief technology officer (CTO)** guides technology strategy; a **chief information officer (CIO)** typically oversees the organisation's information systems and IT investment. An **IT manager** may run a local service team or a much broader portfolio. In a small company, several of these responsibilities may belong to one person. Management is a different set of responsibilities, not the compulsory destination of a technical career.

## Operating services and shared platforms

**Systems administrators** maintain hosts, accounts, patches, backups and recovery. **Unix/Linux administrators** and **Windows administrators** bring platform-specific depth. An operations-oriented **systems engineer** designs and automates those environments. An **infrastructure engineer** works across compute, storage and provisioning; a **virtualisation engineer** specialises in hypervisors and virtual machines. A **cloud engineer** implements and operates cloud resources. A **Kubernetes engineer** focuses on container orchestration, cluster upgrades and workload operations. Tool expertise does not remove the need for recovery planning.

An **operations analyst** examines workload and service performance. A **computer operator** may monitor scheduled processing and carry out controlled operational procedures. A **build and release engineer** makes software builds and packaging repeatable; a **CI/CD or automation engineer** builds delivery workflows, integrations and deployment controls. The release manager coordinates the release decision; these engineers make its execution reliable.

Three nearby labels deserve careful comparison:

- **DevOps engineer**: often automates delivery and infrastructure. DevOps also describes shared development and operations practices across a team, so hiring one person cannot supply all of it. [@dora-capabilities-roles]
- **Site reliability engineer (SRE)**: applies software engineering to reliability, using service level objectives, error budgets, incident learning and reduction of repetitive operational work. Part 3 develops these ideas. [@google-sre]
- **Platform engineer**: builds supported self-service capabilities for other developers, such as an approved way to create and deploy a service. A **platform product manager** investigates internal users' needs and prioritises that platform's roadmap. [@cncf-platform-roles]

The same person may do all three, but the success criteria differ. For Coralline's portal, ask whether a deployment is repeatable, whether users receive the agreed reliability, and whether a second team can use the deployment service without a ticket. A **developer experience engineer** investigates friction across local tools, builds and documentation, which can overlap with platform engineering.

An **observability engineer** makes logs, metrics and traces useful for diagnosis. A **chaos engineer** designs controlled resilience experiments with agreed limits and stop conditions. A **FinOps engineer or analyst** helps engineering, finance and product teams understand usage, allocate cost and improve value; the work includes trade-offs rather than simply cutting the bill. [@finops-framework-roles]

Networks and security cross these boundaries. A **telecoms engineer** works on communications systems; a **network administrator** maintains network configuration and access. A **network engineer** builds and troubleshoots connectivity; a **network architect** designs its wider structure. A **cloud network engineer** handles cloud routing, private connectivity and network controls.

A **security analyst** investigates alerts and exposures; a **security engineer** implements protective controls. An **information security officer** coordinates policy and risk, while a **chief information security officer (CISO)** leads the organisation's security programme. A **penetration tester** tests agreed targets under explicit authorisation. An **application security engineer** works with developers on threat modelling and software weaknesses. A **DevSecOps engineer** integrates security checks and controls into delivery. An **AI red teamer** probes AI systems for security failures and harmful behaviour within an agreed test scope. Security remains a shared responsibility. [@microsoft-ai-red-team-roles]

## Data, AI and quality

A **database administrator (DBA)** looks after database access, performance, backup and recovery. A **data engineer** builds reliable data ingestion and processing; an **ETL developer** specialises in extracting, transforming and loading data. A **big data or Hadoop engineer** focuses on distributed processing platforms. A **data warehouse/business intelligence (BI) developer** builds analytical stores and reporting products. An **analytics engineer** makes transformed datasets dependable through modelling, tests and documentation. [@dbt-analytics-roles]

A **data analyst** uses data to answer business questions and explain results. A **statistician** brings depth in study design, inference and uncertainty. A **data scientist** investigates data and develops statistical or predictive approaches. At Coralline, the analyst might explain why returns increased, the analytics engineer would maintain a consistent definition of a return, and the data engineer would keep the source records arriving. A DBA would check that recovery works when the underlying database fails.

A **machine learning engineer** turns models into tested, efficient production components. An **MLOps engineer** supports repeatable training, model and data versioning, deployment, monitoring and rollback. Model behaviour can change when the data changes, even without a code release. [@google-mlops-roles]

An **AI engineer** may assemble applications using existing models, retrieval and tools; the title does not necessarily mean training a foundation model. A **prompt engineer** develops and evaluates instructions and examples. An **AI agent/context engineer** may manage retrieved information, conversation state and tool use across multiple steps. These are emerging, inconsistently scoped titles; prompting and context design can also be tasks inside another job. [@anthropic-context-roles]

For the refund assistant, those engineers must agree evaluation criteria, data access, human approval and a recovery path with the service owner and security team. A good demonstration does not establish permission to move money. The AI red teamer investigates failure paths; the product and service owners remain responsible for release decisions within the organisation's governance.

Quality roles challenge assumptions throughout delivery. A **software tester** investigates behaviour, including unexpected paths. A **QA engineer** helps the team plan and improve quality practices. A **test automation engineer** builds repeatable checks; an **SDET (software development engineer in test)** often builds test frameworks, harnesses and testable interfaces. The titles overlap. Passing automated tests cannot tell you whether you forgot an important user need.

## Customer-facing work and your next move

Priya's **service desk analyst** role covers intake, classification, communication and escalation. A **help desk technician** may concentrate on user faults and device setup. **Technical support** is a broad label; an **application support analyst** investigates a particular application's workflows and data. A **support engineer** may debug product faults and produce reproductions for development. A **customer or field engineer** may install, integrate and repair systems at a customer site. L1, L2 and L3 describe support tiers, rather than universal job grades.

**Sales engineers**, **solutions engineers** and pre-sales **systems engineers** often run discovery, demonstrations and proofs of concept. Their job includes checking technical fit and explaining limitations before promises reach a contract. Solutions engineer can also include implementation work, so check the scope. Part 5 connects these technical roles with account executives and customer success managers.

A **forward deployed engineer (FDE)** works closely with a customer to understand a problem and build working integrations or applications, often using a vendor's platform. Palantir's role description is one concrete example, rather than a definition binding every employer. Agree who maintains the customer-specific code after handover. An **IT consultant** may advise, implement or both under an agreed engagement. [@palantir-fde-roles]

A **technical writer** develops usable instructions, API references and explanations. A **developer advocate**, working in **developer relations (DevRel)**, helps developers use a product through examples, education and feedback to engineering. A **technology evangelist** often emphasises awareness and adoption. These roles may overlap with marketing and engineering, but their credibility depends on understanding users' actual problems. Part 7 adds community governance and cultural responsibilities.

You can enter IT through support, development, testing, data, apprenticeships or graduate programmes. From the desk, Priya can shadow specialists, write useful knowledge-base articles and take on small improvements. She can pursue technical depth or people and service leadership; she can also move sideways. Look for mentoring, decision scope, on-call expectations and opportunities to learn, rather than assuming a title guarantees progression.

Try a handoff map for Coralline's refund assistant. Choose who defines acceptable refund behaviour, builds the interface, provides trusted data, tests unsafe requests, approves release and handles a wrong refund after launch. For each handoff, record an output, the next recipient and the decision authority. In a five-person company, one person may hold several roles. The responsibilities still need an owner.
